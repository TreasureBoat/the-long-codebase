## The pitch I ignored

Paul and I have worked together on projects in the US and in South Africa for years. He
runs a product of his own, but he is not the person who writes framework code for a
living. Around November 2025 he started using Claude at the consulting company he worked
for, on one project after another, and he started telling me about it.

He was not subtle. AI is amazing. You have to try this. He sent me prompts. He told me
about work that would have taken fourteen days being done in an afternoon — six hours,
maybe.

I ignored all of it.

Not out of principle. I was doing ten to fifteen hour days, and I had seen every line of
the code I was responsible for. That is not a boast, it is just what maintaining a
twenty-year-old framework by yourself looks like. When someone tells you a machine will
write it faster, the question is not whether it is faster. The question is whether you
will still know what is in there.

## First contact

The first AI I actually used was GitHub Copilot, because it came with the subscription I
already had.

The first thing I tried it on was Javadoc. That was genuinely good. Nobody enjoys writing
Javadoc — it is necessary, it is repetitive, and it is the first thing to rot. Having a
draft to correct instead of a blank comment to fill was a real improvement, and I
remember thinking there might be something here after all.

Then I went and did something more ambitious.

## The country enum

I was reworking `ETBWCountry`, our country enum. It is exactly the sort of thing that is
tedious rather than difficult: every country on Earth, with codes, in a form the framework
can use.

So I asked Copilot to write it. And it did. It produced a long, clean, plausible enum,
formatted correctly, with no complaints and no caveats.

I was pleased with that for two days.

Then I counted. There were about 160 entries. Depending on how you count, there are
somewhere around 195 countries. It had quietly left out dozens of them.

Not with an error. Not with a note saying the list was partial. It gave me a list that
*looked* complete, in a format where the only way to discover otherwise is to check every
single entry against a real source — which is precisely the work I had been trying to
avoid by asking in the first place.

That was the last day I used Copilot.

## Why Paul's pitch did not land

So when Paul started sending me things about Claude, it landed on a man who had already
been burned in the most specific way possible.

The problem was never that the machine could not write code. It obviously could. The
problem was that it was confidently, silently incomplete, and that verifying its output
cost as much as doing the work myself. For a codebase where a mistake means a Sunday
restoring a database, that trade is not close.

He kept going anyway. I kept developing.

## What actually changed my mind

It was not an argument, and it was not a demo.

Paul knew that I had wanted to get off Eclipse for years. Eclipse is a pain — that is the
polite version — and our whole tooling story was stuck to it. So at the start of December
2025, instead of sending me more prompts, he spent about two weeks building a prototype
IntelliJ plugin for TreasureBoat. He sent the screenshots over Discord as he went.

The prototype was full of bugs. None of the details were finished. By the standards of
something you would ship it was nowhere.

But it worked. The thing I had been putting off for years, because I never had a spare two
weeks, existed well enough to open.

That was the moment. Not "AI can write code" — I already knew that, and I knew what it was
worth. It was that he had done it *from outside*: Paul writes Java, but he had never
written an IntelliJ plugin, he does not know the internals of my framework, and this is a
side project for him on top of a full-time job. Two weeks, in the evenings, and the thing
existed.

## The unglamorous part

In December 2025 I bought my IntelliJ licences and signed up for the free Claude account.
Five minutes, and the tokens were gone.

So I moved to Pro. Still gone. I spent about ten days in a rhythm of working until the
limit, then waiting for the reset, then working again — which tells you something about
how much of it I was suddenly using, from someone who had refused to use any of it a
month earlier.

Then I moved to the Max plan, and we have been working hard together since.

## What I did not stop believing

Here is the part that matters, and it is the reason this blog exists.

**The country enum problem never went away.**

In a single day last week, converting a five-year-old production database, Claude was
confidently wrong four times. It told me a set of constraints did not exist when there
were about a hundred and seventy of them. It built an entire theory about a framework-wide
bug that turned out to be two different databases being compared. Each time it was
articulate, plausible, and wrong in exactly the way that enum was wrong.

What changed is not the machine's reliability. What changed is that I now know what that
failure looks like, I expect it, and the work is structured so it gets caught — and in
exchange I get something that will read eighty-four table definitions against eighteen
models without getting bored, which I will not.

That is a much smaller claim than the one Paul was making at me nine months ago. It is
also the one I would actually defend.
